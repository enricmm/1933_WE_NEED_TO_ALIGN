# We Need to Align
## Inside the European Organisational Theatre

Short satirical essay about European organisational life. The book looks at corporations, consulting, institutions, EU projects and the machinery of coordination from the perspective of someone who has been both employee and embedded consultant. The recurring idea is that organisational absurdity is often produced not by stupid people, but by intelligent people reacting rationally to bad incentives, diffuse responsibility, politics, fear and the need to appear in control.

## Introduction — Welcome to the Organisation

Establish the authorial point of view: insider enough to understand how organisations really work, outsider enough to notice the theatre. Introduce the core idea that the system, not individual stupidity, often creates the absurdity.

## 1. The Meeting

The basic unit of organisational life. Why meetings attract more people than necessary, why an hour becomes the default duration, why decisions are often postponed inside the room and then made afterwards by two people who could have spoken before the meeting.

## 2. We Need to Align

Explore the strange organisational condition in which agreement is not enough: people must also be aligned, visibly aligned and preferably aligned again in a follow-up. A chapter about coordination, political safety and the reluctance to move before everyone has signalled consent.

## 3. The Steering Committee

The body created to govern what nobody fully owns. Sponsors, escalations, collective decisions and shared accountability, with particular attention to how governance can distribute responsibility so widely that nobody can quite be blamed for the outcome.

## 4. The PowerPoint Version of Reality

Reality is messy, contradictory and incomplete; the deck has five pillars, three arrows and a conclusion. Look at how organisations simplify complexity to make it presentable, governable and reassuring, and what gets lost when the representation starts replacing the thing itself.

## 5. The Consultant

Why organisations pay outsiders to say things insiders already know, and why that can sometimes make perfect sense. Also what happens when the supposedly external consultant has been embedded for six months and is now simultaneously inside the system and officially outside it.

## 6. The Transformation Programme

Transformation as a permanent organisational state. New structures, new terminology, new workstreams and a fresh governance model, while many of the underlying problems survive from one programme to the next. The target is not to mock change itself, but the machinery that can make change look more substantial than it is.

## 7. The KPI

What happens when measurement designed to improve reality becomes the reality people optimise. Targets, dashboards and performance indicators as useful tools that can also redirect behaviour toward making the number look right rather than making the underlying system work better.

## 8. The Workshop

Post-its, break-out groups and twenty minutes to brainstorm. Why workshops can be genuinely useful, but also how organisations sometimes use participation rituals to arrive collectively at an answer someone had already written before everyone entered the room.

## 9. The European Project

Partners, work packages, deliverables, dissemination, governance and a remarkable amount of coordination to get several organisations to do one thing together. Use European and transnational projects as a particularly rich example of the tension between necessary coordination and coordination becoming an end in itself.

## 10. Nobody Owns the Problem

Everyone is a stakeholder; nobody is responsible. Examine what happens when ownership is fragmented across functions, committees and organisations, and how sensible local behaviour can produce collective paralysis.

## 11. The Organisation Chart

How to draw an organisation so that it appears to work in a way nobody who works there recognises. Contrast formal reporting lines with the real networks of influence, knowledge, trust and decision-making through which work actually gets done.

## 12. Corporate European English

The language that allows people to disagree without disagreeing, say no without saying no and postpone a decision while remaining constructive. Satirical translations can appear as a recurring device, but the chapter is really about language as organisational risk management and political signalling.

## 13. The Reorganisation

When the answer to a problem is to move the boxes. Why reorganisations are attractive because they are visible, controllable and announce action, and why they so often leave incentives, processes, power relationships and practical problems largely untouched.

## 14. The Leadership Problem

Vision, empowerment, ownership and accountability are universally admired until they require an uncomfortable decision. Look at the gap between leadership language and the institutional incentives that make real delegation, responsibility and risk-taking difficult.

## 15. The Actual Work

Return to the people who, between meetings, governance, reporting, alignment and transformation, still try to do the work that justifies the organisation’s existence. This chapter should bring the satire back to its human centre: most people are not the problem; they are trying to function inside the system.

## Conclusion — Perfectly Aligned

Do not end with management lessons or a list of fixes. Close with a more human observation: these structures usually exist for understandable reasons, but when an organisation spends too much time managing itself, the theatre begins to consume the work it was meant to support.
